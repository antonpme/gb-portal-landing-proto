Design system

Icons

A glyph that answers the pointer is a button. A glyph that does not is an icon. Both are on this page, in the same order of sections as every other component card, and every number below is read back out of the rendered page at your current window width. Click any specimen on a stage or a shelf to open its properties.

Window width right now: measuring. Neither scale steps with the window: a target big enough for a thumb is big enough for a mouse.

Component

Icons and icon buttons Draft

One drawing grid, one stroke, one ink rule, and two objects: the glyph that stands in a block and the round control that holds one.

Overview

The icon

A box with a drawing in it. It has a size and an ink and nothing else: no hover, no cursor of its own, no target. It takes its colour from whatever sentence, row or button it stands in, which is why every glyph in the house is inline markup drawn in currentColor and not a picture file.

StatusDraft, 28 August. Built for feedback: no consumer has been converted yet

The icon button

The round control with a glyph and no label. It is not a component of its own: it is the --icon shape of the button organism, so it keeps the type, the colour, the five states and the focus ring the organism already has, and only the box changes. Four flat rungs, and the glyph is always half the circle.

States

What changes, and to what

The table follows the stage: it is filled by measuring the five specimens standing above it, so it always describes the variant on the stage and never a general case. A washed circle answers with its ground, and its ink holds still. A plain one has no ground to answer with, so everything it has to say has to be said in the ink, and the table shows exactly where that leaves each colour family.

StateGroundInkHairlineToken

A finding, and a question with it. The organism holds one ink at rest and at hover for secondary and for inverse, because that is what live measures on the header: the circle answers with its wash and the glyph does not move. So a plain glyph in those two families does not move at all, and the only family with a hover ink of its own is primary. The quiet glyph row that the portal bar wears by hand, Zinc 500 darkening to Zinc 700, is a fourth colour family the organism does not have. Adding one is a decision for Ton, and until it is taken, plain belongs where the row around it owns the ink.

Sizes

Two scales: the glyph and the circle

The glyph scale is what an icon measures on its own. The circle scale is what a control measures around one. They are separate because they answer separate questions, and they meet in one rule: the glyph is half the circle, which is the ratio live already draws.

The glyph scale, and the shelf below it is live: click any of the four.

Usage

Which one, and when

One question decides it before any other: does this glyph answer the pointer? If it does it is a button and it needs a name, a target and a tooltip. If it does not it is an icon, and it must not look like it might.

Reach forWhenNot when
IconA mark that belongs to a line of text, a row or a block: it carries meaning and never a clickAnywhere a person could reasonably try to press it. A glyph that looks pressable and is not is the most expensive thing a page can contain
Icon buttonA control whose meaning is universal and whose room is tight: a close, a step back, a search, a basketFor an action the reader has to think about. A word costs one line and saves a guess
Icon button, staticThe same circle standing in a block as decoration, so a mark and a control look like one familyAs a substitute for a disabled control. Disabled is a state of a control and keeps its name; static is not a control at all
WashedA single glyph in a bar or a head, where the ground appearing under the pointer is the whole answerIn a tight row of glyphs, where a plate under each one turns the row into a fence
PlainA row of glyphs whose own ink is quiet enough to darken, so the row answers without changing shapeWhere the ink at rest is already the darkest it goes: nothing will happen under the pointer, and a control that does not answer reads as broken
GhostPermanent chrome, which is nearly every round glyph in the productFor the action a screen is asking for. That action deserves a word and a filled button
OutlineA round control that has to read as a control on a busy or photographic groundOn a quiet white plate, where the hairline is louder than the job
40A dense toolbar, where a 44 circle would not fit the bandAs the only control in a wide empty area, and never on a touch surface without room around it
44The default, and what every bar and drawer head in the product measures todayInside a 36 high band, where it will not fit
48 and 56A single round action carrying a block of its ownIn a row with other glyphs, where one big circle reads as an error rather than an emphasis

A glyph is not a word. Reach for a label whenever the meaning is not already universal, and never invent a picture for an idea the product has not already taught: the reader is not going to learn a private alphabet for one screen. Where a glyph does stand alone, it takes the name of the action and not the name of the drawing: close, not cross.

Accessibility
NameAn interactive glyph carries aria-label, always. Without it a screen reader announces a button and nothing else, and the drawer on this page prints the name in red words when it is missing
SilenceA decorative glyph carries aria-hidden="true" and no name at all. Two names for one thing, one spoken and one drawn, is worse than none
TooltipA control with no label needs a hover and focus tooltip as well as a name: the name serves the screen reader, the tooltip serves the person who can see the glyph and still does not know it
TargetWCAG 2.2 asks 44 by 44, and the default circle is exactly that. 40 clears it only with room around it; the anatomy table above says which rung passes on its own
KeyboardThe focus ring is the organism's: :focus-visible, two pixels at two pixels offset, white instead of blue on a dark ground. It follows the circle, because an outline follows the radius
ContrastA glyph is a graphical object and has to clear 3 to 1 against its ground, not the 4.5 of text. Both figures on this page are measured off the rendered page rather than remembered
Static is not disabledA static glyph is invisible to the keyboard and to the reader by design. A control that is temporarily unavailable is disabled and keeps both
Code

Four shapes, and the drawing is inline in every one of them. That is what makes the ink free: a glyph in markup is currentColor, and a glyph in an img is whatever grey it was exported with.

<!-- an icon in a block: 20 is the default rung -->
<span class="gb-icon gb-icon--20" aria-hidden="true">
  <svg viewBox="0 0 24 24">...</svg>
</span>

<!-- the chrome control: the live circle, ghost, black -->
<button class="gb-btn gb-btn--icon gb-btn--ghost gb-btn--secondary"
        type="button" aria-label="Close" title="Close">
  <span class="gb-btn__icon"><svg viewBox="0 0 24 24">...</svg></span>
</button>

<!-- the same control on a dark ground, one rung smaller -->
<button class="gb-btn gb-btn--icon gb-btn--s gb-btn--ghost gb-btn--inverse"
        type="button" aria-label="Search gifts" title="Search gifts">
  <span class="gb-btn__icon"><svg viewBox="0 0 24 24">...</svg></span>
</button>

<!-- decoration: a span, no name, no target -->
<span class="gb-btn gb-btn--icon gb-btn--static gb-btn--ghost gb-btn--secondary"
      aria-hidden="true">
  <span class="gb-btn__icon"><svg viewBox="0 0 24 24">...</svg></span>
</span>

The set is a record and not a sprite. window.GbIcons hands back the drawing of a named glyph, or the whole span around it, so a page can write GbIcons.html('close', 20) instead of pasting a path; a sprite would cost a request and take the ink away. Every consumer keeps writing inline markup, and what changes is that the drawing has one home instead of nine.
